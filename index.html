Where regulators start asking institutions to prove what a system decided.

One living picture across six jurisdictions and twenty-four regulators, refreshed every Monday from primary sources. The question is narrow: at what point does a rule, a consultation or an enforcement action require someone to reconstruct and defend a particular automated decision after the fact?

Last swept: 31 August 2026 · next sweep Monday 7 September, 07:00 Europe/Berlin

State of play

What is currently open, live, or awaited

Each block holds the current position for that jurisdiction — open consultations, live obligations, and anything awaited. Superseded items are removed rather than archived; this is a picture of now, not a pile of reports.

South Africa Analysed

FSCA · Prudential Authority · SARB · National Credit Regulator · Information Regulator
  • Still awaited — the FSCA–PA discussion paper. Announced as the next step in the November 2025 joint report. Nine months on, not published. Checked this week: FSCA Documents for Consultation (2026 items are capital markets, CIS, FAIS and fintech — nothing on AI), Publications and Resources (most recent discussion paper is ESG rating services, 31 March 2026), Latest News (nothing of any kind since 2 July 2026), and the PA publications and consultation listings. The PA’s own chapter in the SARB Annual Report 2025/26 records the AI survey as delivered and mentions no forthcoming discussion paper. No published timetable. Highest-priority watch item.
  • POPIA s71 is live — and entirely unelaborated. Automated loan rejection requires human review and an appeal mechanism; the data subject may make representations and must receive “sufficient information about the underlying logic.” Verified this week that the Information Regulator has issued no guidance note touching s71 (its six notes cover direct marketing, voters and misinformation, special information, children, COVID-19, and political-party voter data), has never issued an s71 enforcement notice, and has published no media statement on automated decision-making or profiling. The obligation exists; the interpretation does not.
  • FAIS automated-advice standards apply technology-neutrally to robo-advisors: algorithm monitoring, review and testing, internal controls, system governance.
  • PA communication, 24 April 2026 — Preparing for Frontier AI and AI Accelerated Cyber Risk. The PA’s own detail page gives the purpose as “to raise awareness of a material shift in the cyber-risk landscape arising from rapid advances in artificial intelligence (AI).” The underlying PDF would not open — HTTP 403, four attempts across two URL casings — so nothing is recorded here about what it says. Logged as an open gap, not as a finding.
  • Read together: no live South African rule, consultation, enforcement action or supervisory statement names a moment at which a firm must reconstruct or prove what a particular automated system did or decided. POPIA s71 is the only obligation of that character in South African law, and it stands without guidance, precedent or consultation behind it.

European Union Analysed

European Commission AI Office · EBA · EIOPA · ESMA · EDPB · ECB / SSM
  • The high-risk regime has been formally deferred — the material change this week. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of 8 July 2026, amending Regulations (EU) 2024/1689, (EU) 2018/1139 and (EU) 2023/1230 as regards the simplification of the implementation of harmonised rules on artificial intelligence — the “Digital Omnibus on AI” — entered into force 27 July 2026 and amends Article 113. Per the Commission’s own announcement: “High-risk AI systems in Annex III: Rules apply starting 2 December 2027”, and Annex I systems from 2 August 2028. So the Chapter III obligations on credit scoring under Annex III 5(b) now bite from 2 December 2027, not 2 August 2026. This is enacted law, not a proposal.
  • Article 86 was not amended, and the two dates have come apart. Regulation 2026/1744 does not mention Article 86 or the right to explanation, and does not amend Article 12 or Article 26. Article 86 sits outside Chapter III, so its 2 August 2026 application date stands on its face — while the Chapter III obligations of the very deployers it refers to are now deferred to December 2027. Recorded as a fact about two dates, not as a conclusion about the consequence. Article 6 was also amended, inserting 6(1a): AI systems “solely used for non-safety related aspects of user assistance, performance optimisation, service efficiency, automation or convenience or quality control shall not qualify as safety components”.
  • Credit scoring is high-risk, by name. The AI Act (Reg. EU 2024/1689) lists at Annex III point 5(b) “AI systems intended to be used to evaluate the creditworthiness of natural persons or establish their credit score, with the exception of AI systems used for the purpose of detecting financial fraud”. Life and health insurance risk assessment and pricing is the second financial high-risk use case.
  • Article 86 — right to explanation of individual decision-making, applicable since 2 August 2026. An affected person subject to a decision taken by the deployer on the output of an Annex III high-risk system, which produces legal effects or similarly significantly affects them adversely, “shall have the right to obtain from the deployer clear and meaningful explanations of the role of the AI system in the decision-making procedure and the main elements of the decision taken.” A per-decision, after-the-fact obligation — not a model-level disclosure. Note Article 86(3): the Article “shall apply only to the extent that the right referred to in paragraph 1 is not otherwise provided for under Union law” — which is why the EBA rates the consumer right to explanation as already fully aligned with the Consumer Credit Directive, and why Article 86 bites hardest outside consumer credit.
  • Article 12 — the logs must exist. High-risk systems “shall technically allow for the automatic recording of events (logs) over the lifetime of the system”, with logging capabilities enabling “a level of traceability of the functioning of a high-risk AI system that is appropriate to the intended purpose”.
  • Article 26(6) — and be kept. Deployers keep the automatically generated logs, to the extent under their control, “for a period appropriate to the intended purpose of the high-risk AI system, of at least six months”. Second subparagraph, directly on point for regulated firms: “Deployers that are financial institutions subject to requirements regarding their internal governance, arrangements or processes under Union financial services law shall maintain the logs as part of the documentation kept pursuant to the relevant Union financial service law.”
  • Article 26(11) — and the person must be told. Deployers of high-risk systems making or assisting decisions about natural persons must inform them they are subject to its use.
  • EBA mapping exercise, 21 November 2025. Mapped AI Act high-risk requirements against CRD, CRR, DORA, the Consumer Credit and Mortgage Credit Directives, PSD and EBA Guidelines, focused specifically on creditworthiness and credit scoring. Found no significant contradictions; the AI Act is complementary to existing banking law. No new EBA Guidelines planned — supervisory-cooperation work instead, through 2026/2027.
  • ECB/SSM Supervisory Newsletter, 20 November 2025 — AI’s impact on banking: use cases for credit scoring and fraud detection. Of the banks sampled, “none… permits self-learning after deployment” specifically to keep models “stable and auditable”; about half have dedicated AI policies or committees; “a few banks seem to lack full transparency regarding the internal processes of some AI models.”
  • The EBA’s own mapping table is the finding. Fourteen AI Act obligations scored against CRR/CRD, DORA, CCD, MCD, PSD and the EBA Loan Origination Guidelines. Exactly one cell in the entire matrix is rated fully aligned: consumer right to explanation, against the Consumer Credit Directive. Everywhere else that row is grey — “not present in Financial Services Law.” EU financial law contains a right to an explanation in one place only: consumer credit. Not in prudential rules, not in mortgage credit, not in payments, not in the loan origination guidelines.
  • And the machinery to honour it is rated as missing or extended. In the same table, record-keeping is complementary against CRR/CRD and DORA — the EBA’s definition being that the AI Act “extends or adds new dimension/objective to FS law requirements” — and grey against CCD, MCD and PSD. Human oversight is grey against DORA, MCD, PSD and the EBA Guidelines. Transparency to deployers is grey against CCD, MCD, PSD and the Guidelines. So the right to an explanation is the one thing already covered; producing the evidence to satisfy it is not.
  • Awaited: the Article 6(5) high-risk classification guidelines. Still not adopted. The 2 February 2026 statutory deadline was missed and was not itself amended by the omnibus. A draft was published 19 May 2026 and a targeted stakeholder consultation closed 23 July 2026; the Commission states feedback “is going to be incorporated in the final version of the guidelines before adoption”, and that they “will be published ahead of the application date for these rules” — now December 2027. No calendar date given.
  • Commission / AI Office, 20 July 2026 — Guidelines on the Article 50 transparency obligations, applicable from 2 August 2026. Providers “will have to add machine-readable marks to enable the detection of AI-generated or manipulated content” — a duty to make after-the-fact identification of what a system produced technically possible. Provenance rather than reasoning, but it is the same evidentiary instinct.
  • Open now: EBA consultation on RTS on the Operational Risk Management Framework, 26 August 2026 (EBA/CP/2026/18). Identifies “risks arising from the increasing use of artificial intelligence and other new technologies.” Article 12(2)(b) requires assessment of model soundness “where models are used for decision-making purposes, such as for product pricing, artificial intelligence applications, the evaluation of financial instruments or client profiling”, and Article 15(3) requires “minimum data quality controls, such as audit trails.” Model risk and data governance, not a per-decision duty. Closes 31 December 2026.
  • Commission targeted consultation on AI in the financial sector closed 13 September 2024; responses fed the work above.

United Kingdom Analysed

FCA · Bank of England / PRA · ICO
  • The statutory hook is now live: UK GDPR Article 22C, in force 5 February 2026. Section 80 of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 replaced the old Article 22 prohibition with a permissive regime plus mandatory safeguards, commenced by SI 2026/82 reg 2(j). For significant solely-automated decisions the controller must “provide the data subject with information about decisions described in paragraph 1 taken in relation to the data subject”, “enable the data subject to make representations about such decisions”, “enable the data subject to obtain human intervention on the part of the controller in relation to such decisions”, and “enable the data subject to contest such decisions.” A duty to inform about a decision already taken, and to support contest and human re-review of it. Prospective only: reg 5 saves decisions taken before 5 February 2026.
  • The ICO has said in terms that this needs an audit trail. Its ADM safeguards guidance: controllers should “use a system that is able to deliver an audit trail showing the key decision points that formed the basis for the decision”, must “explain all ADM to give people an understanding of how you reached the decision”, and human reviewers must “have discretion and authority to alter the decision” and “take into account the relevant data and factors that the decision was based on.” This is the clearest per-decision reconstruction expectation of any regulator swept so far.
  • Awaited: the final ICO guidance. The consultation on draft ADM and profiling guidance opened 31 March 2026 and closed 29 May 2026. No consultation response published. Final guidance targeted Winter 2026 per the ICO’s own guidance plan.
  • Awaited: a statutory code of practice on AI and ADM. SI 2026/425, in force 12 May 2026, requires the Information Commissioner to prepare “an appropriate code of practice giving guidance as to good practice in the processing of personal data” relating to AI and automated decision-making. The ICO’s plan of action commits to cover “transparency and explainability, bias and discrimination and rights and redress.” No publication date set. The Regulations themselves impose no explanation duty.
  • Enforcement: FCA fines Dinosaur Merchant Bank £338,000, Final Notice 24 March 2026. Final Notice · press release. Some $3.05bn of CFD orders and trades “were not captured and reviewed by the automated surveillance system.” The failings are squarely evidentiary: “Alert scenarios which had never triggered alerts were not reviewed to check whether they were indeed switched on, receiving the relevant data and were working as expected”; “There was no written record documenting the manual surveillance undertaken”; and a failure to obtain management information “sufficient to enable its functioning to be effectively reviewed and any issues or concerns identified, challenged and any remedial measures proposed or monitored.” A firm fined because it could not show what its automated system was doing.
  • Bank of England AI Consortium, minutes of 3 June 2026 (published 5 August 2026). Workshop on “Explainability and Transparency in GenAI” adopts an “outcomes-based definition focussed on understanding decision-making and whether systems are behaving as intended”, with mitigants including “human in the loop (HiTL), adversarial testing, and accountability and auditable documentation of decisions.” A Consortium report on this work is due during 2026.
  • Awaited: the FCA’s AI good and poor practice publication, committed to in the Mills Review of 6 July 2026 for “later this year.” Nikhil Rathi’s 24 June 2026 speech on agentic AI goes no further than “accountability for regulated activities and outcomes must remain clear” — posture, not requirement.
  • Verified negative: the Bank’s July 2026 Financial Stability Report treats AI opacity as an exposure problem — “it may be difficult for financial firms to be aware of the full extent of their direct and indirect exposures to the AI ecosystem” — not a decision-auditability one. Recorded so it is not mistaken for support.

United States Active

CFPB · FTC · Federal Reserve · OCC · NAIC
  • The direction here is subtraction, and that is the finding. The two US instruments that most directly required a lender to explain a black-box decision, and the decade-old federal model-documentation standard, have all been withdrawn or softened. What survives is one regulation and one state-level bulletin.
  • Both CFPB adverse-action circulars are rescinded, effective 12 May 2025. 90 FR doc 2025-08286 withdrew 67 guidance documents, naming both “Circular 2022-03: Adverse action notification requirements in connection with credit decisions based on complex algorithms” and “Circular 2023-03: Adverse action notification requirements and the proper use of the CFPB’s sample forms provided in Regulation B.” The circulars index now shows one live circular, on credit card rewards. The interpretation that a creditor using a black-box model could not fall back on generic reasons is no longer Bureau guidance.
  • What still binds: 12 CFR 1002.9, Regulation B. § 1002.9(b)(2): “The statement of reasons for adverse action required by paragraph (a)(2)(i) of this section must be specific and indicate the principal reason(s) for the adverse action” — and a statement that the applicant failed to achieve a qualifying score, or citing internal standards alone, is insufficient. Confirmed not amended in 2026: the 22 April 2026 final rule (effective 21 July 2026) touched §§ 1002.4, 1002.6, 1002.8, 1002.15 and Supplement I only, removing effects-test language. This is now the only hard federal obligation of its kind for credit — the text binds, the guidance explaining how it applies to opaque models does not.
  • SR 11-7 is gone. Superseded 17 April 2026. OCC Bulletin 2026-13 / Fed SR 26-2, Model Risk Management: Revised Guidance, rescinds OCC Bulletin 2011-12 and supersedes SR 11-7. The old standard — documentation “sufficiently detailed to allow parties unfamiliar with a model to understand how the model operates, as well as its limitations and key assumptions” — does not survive; the replacement is discretionary: “Adequate documentation helps to support effective model risk management.” Two express carve-outs: “Generative AI and agentic AI models are novel and rapidly evolving. As such, they are not within the scope of this guidance”, and the guidance “does not set forth enforceable standards or prescriptive requirements; accordingly, non-compliance with this guidance will not result in supervisory criticism.” The third-party-reconstruction test is the specific thing that was removed.
  • Awaited, and overdue — the interagency AI request for information. Bulletin 2026-13 states “the agencies plan to issue in the near future a request for information that addresses model risk management generally and considers, in particular, banks’ use of AI, including generative AI and agentic AI and AI-based models.” Not issued as at 31 August 2026. Separately, Fed Vice Chair for Supervision Bowman’s 1 May 2026 speech flagged a consultation draft on international AI adoption sound practices for “the third quarter” — also unissued. Both would be priority-1 items the moment they land.
  • Open now: FTC proposed enforcement policy statement on personalized pricing, 19 August 2026. Proposed statement (matter P034101). Requires firms to “clearly and conspicuously disclose not just that the price is personalized, but also the basis for that personalization and the types of data on which the personalization is based.” Comment period is 30 days from Federal Register publication; the FR notice could not be located, so the exact closing date is unconfirmed. Forward-looking disclosure of the basis, not after-the-fact reconstruction of a particular price.
  • Enforcement with a retention duty: FTC finalises the “Active Listening” orders, 27 August 2026. CMG Media ($880,000), MindSift and 1010 Digital Works ($25,000 each) for falsely claiming an AI service could target ads from conversations captured by smart devices — it was not in fact based on voice data. The order requires records to be created for 20 years and retained 5, including the materials relied on and evidence contradicting the representations, plus a compliance report “sworn under penalty of perjury.” The clearest live US moment where a firm must produce evidence about what an AI-marketed system actually did.
  • The one body still building the machinery is the NAIC. The Model Bulletin on the Use of Artificial Intelligence Systems by Insurers is adopted in 24 states and jurisdictions per the implementation map dated 1 April 2026. Section 4 provides that an insurer “can expect to be asked about its development, deployment, and use of AI Systems” and that the Department may request “Inventories and descriptions of Predictive Models, and AI Systems”, “Documentation of compliance with all applicable AI Program policies” and “Documentation related to validation, testing, and auditing”; § 4.2 requires third-party contracts to “Provide audit rights and/or entitle the Insurer to receive audit reports by qualified auditing entities.” Note the limit: the production duty runs to the regulator, not to the consumer — there is no duty to explain an individual decision. Watch the AI Systems Evaluation Tool v4.0, piloted by 12 states, with adoption anticipated at the 2026 Fall National Meeting.

Australia Analysed

APRA · ASIC · OAIC
  • APRA letter to all regulated entities, April 2026 — the strongest Australian signal. Following a targeted deep-dive on selected large banks, insurers and superannuation trustees in late 2025. APRA’s finding: “assurance practices are not keeping pace with the scale, speed and complexity of AI”, and while most entities recognise existing prudential standards apply, “few have operationalised governance in practice.”
  • Point-in-time assurance is named as the failure. APRA “observed reliance on point in time and sample based assurance methods, despite these methods being ill suited to probabilistic models that learn, adapt and degrade over time. Few entities had continuous validation or monitoring in place to detect issues such as model drift, bias, failure modes, or control breakdowns in a timely manner.” Expectation: “Monitoring should be continuous and proportionate to the criticality of the use case.”
  • Assurance functions cannot currently do the work. “APRA observed internal audit and risk functions are challenged. Many lack the specialist skills and tools required to engage in AI assessment or audit. This is particularly true where agentic behaviour, automated decision making or AI assisted code generation were involved. As a result, assurance activities often lagged AI deployment.”
  • Auditability is pushed into contracts. On suppliers: “limited evidence of specific provisions addressing audit rights, model updates and deviations, incident notification or changes to data handling.” Expectation: “contractual and governance arrangements which provide sufficient transparency, auditability and assurance over AI services”, and entities “should have the ability to understand model behaviour, material changes, performance issues and outcomes… across the service lifecycle.”
  • Accountability across the lifecycle, and humans on high-risk decisions. Minimum governance expectations include “ownership and accountability across the AI lifecycle, from design and development through to deployment, monitoring and decommissioning”, “an inventory of AI tooling and AI use cases”, and “human involvement for high-risk decisions and accountability”.
  • Enforcement is explicit. “Where entities fail to adequately identify, manage or control AI risks in a manner proportionate to their size, scale and complexity, we will take stronger supervisory action and, where appropriate, pursue enforcement.” APRA is “currently finalising its forward plan with regards to supervision of AI risks” — watch for it.
  • ASIC, Corporate Plan 2026–27 (published 26 August 2026) — and note what dropped out. The “poor use of AI” enforcement framing of the 2025–26 plan is not carried forward in those terms. The 2026–27 emphasis is monitoring and market integrity: ASIC will “monitor increased use of AI by banks to identify harms or risks to consumers” and “assess and address AI-driven manipulation and market integrity risks, including deepfakes and misinformation.” The only assurance language is internal — ASIC’s own “AI Policy and AI Assurance Framework” and AI Board, governing its own use.
  • APRA and ASIC joint statement on frontier AI, 27 August 2026. Media release and information paper following roundtables in June and July 2026. The useful phrasing is a demonstrate-it expectation: entities should be able to show that “key decisions, escalation pathways, recovery arrangements, assurance activities and governance processes can operate at the speed required by emerging frontier AI threats.” Also: “Governed, measurable and scalable defensive AI capability remains limited”, and “poorly governed defensive AI introduces new risks, even when deployed with good intentions.” Adjacent to per-decision explainability, not the same thing.
  • OAIC opened. The Privacy Act ADM obligation commences 10 December 2026 — and it is narrower than it is usually reported to be. Part 15 of the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 inserts APP 1.7–1.9, commencing the day after 24 months from Royal Assent of 10 December 2024. Where an entity has arranged for a computer program to make, or do something substantially and directly related to making, a decision that could reasonably be expected to significantly affect an individual’s rights or interests, it must disclose in its privacy policy the kinds of personal information used and the kinds of decisions involved. That is a category-level, ex-ante transparency duty. It is not an individual right to an explanation and it does not require reconstructing a particular decision — recorded explicitly because it is widely mis-cited as an explainability requirement. OAIC guidance was consulted on via an Issues Paper (18 May – 15 June 2026) and is still pending, so the obligation commences in about fourteen weeks with no final regulator guidance. No OAIC determination or enforcement action turning on an automated decision was found.
  • OAIC on the public sector, 21 January 2026 — Automated decision-making and public reporting under the FOI Act. Desktop review of 23 Commonwealth agencies: only 4 disclosed ADM use in their Information Publication Scheme, and 74% gave no identifiable ADM information at all. Recommends agencies publish “a list of decisions they use ADM for” and “the statute that grants them this power.” A proactive disclosure duty, public sector only.
  • Awaited: APRA’s AI supervision forward plan. Still not published, four months on from the April 2026 letter’s “APRA is currently finalising its forward plan with regards to supervision of AI risks.” APRA’s Supervision Priorities page remains dated January 2020 with no AI content. The Corporate Plan 2026–27 (19 August 2026) goes only to “we are expecting entities using AI to strengthen governance, risk management, operational resilience and board oversight.” In remarks of 24 June 2026 APRA Member Therese McCarthy Hockey disclosed a completed targeted review of 11 entities finding governance lags deployment “in decision-critical and operationally embedded applications”, and said “over the coming year, we intend to step up our supervisory engagement on this issue too.” An intention, not a plan.
  • Read together: APRA does not create a consumer right to an explanation — it is prudential, not conduct. What it does create is a supervisory expectation of continuous evidence about model behaviour, held by an entity that can be examined on it, with enforcement attached. That is an obligation to be able to show what a system did, without using those words. OAIC now swept: the Australian transparency duty is a privacy-policy disclosure, not a right to an explanation of your own decision.

Global bodies Analysed

FSB · BIS / BCBS · IOSCO · IAIS · OECD
  • Standing caveat: none of these bodies makes binding law. Everything below is a recommendation, principle, application paper or staff analysis. Obligation arises only where a national or regional authority implements it. No AI consultation is currently open at any of the five.
  • Awaited, and the most consequential item in this block — the FSB final Sound Practices for Responsible Adoption of Artificial Intelligence. The consultation report of 10 June 2026 set out 12 draft sound practices; it closed 22 July 2026 and the roughly 170 written responses were published on 6 August 2026 with no feedback statement attached. The FSB “expects to publish the final report in the coming months.” It will be the first cross-sectoral global text on this.
  • What the FSB draft asks for. Institutions should “maintain accurate documentation of AI use cases” across the lifecycle, capturing model rationale, assumptions, validation findings and “relevant information from third-party AI providers”, with documentation recording “allocation of accountability (i.e. key roles and responsibilities).” For generative and agentic AI it goes further: “prompt versioning and version control to enable rollback” and “chain-of-thought logging to help explain outputs”, plus “root-cause analysis and post-incident reviews for AI-related incidents” — an explicit after-the-fact reconstruction moment. Practice 8 is explainability and transparency; that is where a proof-of-decision expectation would crystallise. Bowman’s 7 July 2026 outreach remarks signal a materiality-tiered final standard: “lower-risk uses of AI should receive a lighter supervisory and regulatory touch.”
  • IOSCO, 25 May 2026 — Supervisory Toolkit for AI Use in Capital Markets (FR/02/2026). Final report; a toolkit for securities regulators, organised around risk materiality. The most explicit evidentiary language of any item here. Table 2 lists as supervisory expectations “AI system decision and usage logs and audit trail documentation, including inputs, outputs, and AI system logic” and “Recordkeeping policies & procedures for AI systems and data, including data with a third-party or external provider”, with “Lack of explainability of AI logic” flagged as a supervisory concern. Chapter 3.1: firms should “maintain comprehensive audit trails to enable supervisory review.” Section 6 scales it: “the extent of transparency and explainability required of an AI use case, system or model according to its assessed risk materiality.”
  • IAIS, July 2025 — Application Paper on the supervision of artificial intelligence (adopted; note the domain is now iais.org). Frames the duty as outcome explanation: para 69, “supervisors should require that insurers are able to meaningfully explain the outcomes of the AI systems that they use.” On provenance, para 52: supervisors “should assess how insurers implement mechanisms that can track data sources used in training” for “reproducibility and traceability.” Para 90: policies “should provide for documentation of the outputs of AI systems, as well as for documentation of the results of any fairness testing performed.” Para 45 requires defining responsibility across the full lifecycle including decommissioning.
  • BIS Financial Stability Institute, December 2024 — Regulating AI in the financial sector (FSI Insights No 63). Staff paper, not a standard — its own disclaimer says the views “do not necessarily reflect those of the BIS or the Basel-based committees.” The most granular list of records to retain anywhere in this block, at para 29: “model changes and audit logs (who did what, and when)”; “preliminary assessments; usage details (such as databases accessed and if data matched verified identities); trails to support AI system outcomes; project documentation”; and “various versions of the model code; and the original data set used to develop, retrain and recalibrate the model.” Para 28 names a customer-facing moment: “an explanation about the decision, including the ‘logic’ or ‘rationale’ of the decisions” in “plain and easy-to-understand information.”
  • The gap: the Basel Committee has issued no AI supervisory expectations. The only dedicated BCBS item is the Newsletter on artificial intelligence and machine learning of 16 March 2022, which states on its face that it “does not constitute new supervisory guidance or expectations”, while flagging future examination of “AI/ML model governance structures, including responsibilities and accountability for AI/ML-driven decisions.” Four years on, nothing has followed. For a page about where proof obligations come from, the absence of a Basel standard is itself the finding.
  • Verified negative: the BIS Annual Economic Report 2026 (28 June 2026) covers AI only in Chapter I, and macroeconomically — the capex surge and its market consequences. Nothing on supervision, explainability or accountability of AI systems.
  • Citation only, not read: OECD, 26 January 2026, Supervision of artificial intelligence in finance: Challenges, policies and practices. Title, date and scope confirmed from the OECD landing page; the full PDF is blocked by robots.txt, so its body text is unread and nothing is characterised here. Needs a manual read.

Industry evidence, kept separate from regulator evidence. AI for Better Insurance (CSIRO for the Insurance Council of Australia, August 2025) is an industry body’s report, not a regulator’s. It is filed here as a priority-2 signal — firms stating their unsolved problems on the record — and never cited as an obligation.

What the industry says it cannot currently do, in its own words: automation creates “a ‘black box’ scenario where insurers are responsible for outcomes without a deep understanding of the AI model’s operations”; there is “difficulty maintaining transparency in decisions, especially when justifying rejections”, which “would require robust solutions for dispute resolution for decisions made with AI assistance”; and AI systems “often lack transparency, complicating oversight and limiting consumers’ ability to contest decisions.”

The guardrails the report proposes for its own use cases are “auditable processes: establish transparent, auditable AI compliance frameworks” and “regulatory acceptance: ensures the AFCA and regulators support AI system outputs”. AFCA is the Australian financial ombud — the body that asks firms to prove what happened. The industry has named the ombud moment as the thing it must solve for, before any regulator required it to.

Voices

Who is shaping this conversation

Sorted by what each does to the argument, not by geography. A regulator changes what is required; a commentator changes the vocabulary buyers use to describe the problem; a buyer signs. The engagement is different for each, and the mistake is treating the list as one list.

Public professional positions and published output only. Most of this conversation happens on LinkedIn, which the weekly sweep cannot read — following and commenting stays manual.

Priority note. On the evidence now on this page, the United Kingdom is not a fast-moving jurisdiction — it is the one that has already moved. UK GDPR Article 22C has been in force since 5 February 2026, carrying a per-decision duty to inform, allow representations, provide human intervention and enable contest; the ICO expects “a system that is able to deliver an audit trail showing the key decision points that formed the basis for the decision”. The EU’s equivalent machinery for credit scoring now arrives 2 December 2027. Australia has supervisory expectation without a consumer right. South Africa has POPIA s71 with almost no auditing capacity behind it. The live, in-force, per-decision obligation is British.

Gatekeepers Decide what counts

They change what is required. Do not comment at them — cite them.
  • Therese McCarthy Hockey — Member, APRA (AU). Signed the April 2026 letter to industry on AI. The strongest single source in the Australian pitch.
  • Nolwazi Hlophe and Lebogang Mabetha — authors, Artificial Intelligence in the South African Financial Sector, FSCA–PA, November 2025. The forthcoming discussion paper is likely to come from the same team.
  • AFCA (AU) and the ombud / tribunal layer generally — not rule-makers, but the place where the proof question is actually asked. Under-watched by everyone.
  • Sheldon Mills — Executive Director, FCA (UK). Led the review into the long-term impact of AI on retail financial services, published 6 July 2026 — seven recommendations including enabling “foundations for agentic finance” and building an “AI-enabled agentic supervisory model”. Notably the review does not address explaining, evidencing or contesting AI-driven decisions. That omission is an opening, not a dead end.
  • Ashley Alder — Chair, FCA. Publicly endorsed the review on publication.
  • FCA AI Lab and AI Live Testing (FS25/5, second cohort announced) — not a person, but the most concrete entry point into a regulator anywhere on this page. Firms in live testing must evidence what their systems did, to a supervisor, by design.
  • European AI Office — enforcing with national authorities since 2 August 2026, and now operating a public AI Act complaints tool and an AI Act whistleblower tool: a live channel for individuals to raise AI Act issues directly. No named official fronting it publicly yet.
  • EBA, EIOPA, ESMA, EDPB, ICO, Information Regulator (SA), NCR — institutional. Watch publications, not people, until a named supervisor starts speaking.

Method

Priority order, and why it runs backwards

Most regulatory monitoring leads with final rules. This one ends with them. By the time guidance is final the decision is made and the influence is gone; the useful moment is earlier.

  1. Open consultations and discussion papers. A decision not yet made.
  2. Published consultation responses. Firms stating their unsolved problems on the record.
  3. Enforcement actions and fines involving automated decisions.
  4. Speeches by senior supervisors. Direction before drafting.
  5. Final guidance, circulars and rules. Confirmation, not signal.

The standing question. For every item: does it name a moment where someone must prove what an automated system did or decided? Where it does, the phrasing is quoted verbatim rather than paraphrased. Primary sources only — the regulator’s own site, never a vendor blog or law-firm summary.

Deep dive · South Africa

Everyone agrees accountability matters. Almost nobody audits.

South Africa’s insurers and lenders rank accountability for AI-driven decisions among their highest ethical concerns — and rank the ability to contest one dead last among their constraints. The joint FSCA–PA survey measured both. The distance between them is the finding.

01 — Base

The population is small, and unevenly distributed

The PA and FSCA surveyed the sector from 21 October 2024. Roughly 2,100 institutions responded; 220 of them use AI. Adoption is concentrated almost entirely in banking — every one of the big four has adopted, while insurance and lending sit at 8% each.

Eight lending institutions is not a sample — it is eight companies. Every lending percentage below moves in steps of 12.5. Read that column as anecdote, and the insurance column as evidence.

02 — The gap

Stated concern runs 3–7× ahead of built capability

The survey asked two separate questions. One: which ethical considerations matter most? Accountability for AI-driven decisions scored near the top everywhere. Two: which governance frameworks are actually established? ML auditing came last in every single sector.

Banking's number is the interesting one. Banks are not under-governed — 91% have model development, validation and ongoing monitoring; 73% have model risk management; 82% have a named accountable person. They have built everything except the ability to have an outsider check the work. Validation is a first-party activity. Auditing is not. That distinction is the whole market.

03 — Where it thins out

The further a control sits from the model, the less of it exists

Ranking the four evidence-relevant governance frameworks by how widely each is established shows a clean gradient. Institutions build inward-facing controls — validating their own models — and stop before the outward-facing ones that would let a regulator, a customer or a court examine a specific decision after the fact.

04 — The blind spot

Contestability ranks last on every list

Asked to name the non-regulatory constraints on AI adoption, insurers produced seventeen. "Contestability and recourse mechanism which include redress" finished seventeenth, at 14%. Banking put it last too (18%). So did lending (13%).

This is not a market that has weighed the problem and priced it. It is a market that has not yet noticed it.

Which is awkward, because it is already law. The report's own Part 2 sets out what POPIA section 71 requires of an automated decision with legal or substantial effect:

“in loan applications, automated approval is permitted, but rejections require additional safeguards like human review and an appeal mechanism to ensure compliance and fairness.”FSCA–PA, Artificial Intelligence in the South African Financial Sector, Part 2

Data subjects may “make representations about the decision” and must receive “sufficient information about the underlying logic” of the process. That is a duty to reconstruct a specific past decision on demand — in force now, not proposed. The constraint insurers rank seventeenth is the one already binding them.

05 — Instrumentation

Nearly a third of insurers can explain nothing

Explainability is the mechanism by which the POPIA duty gets discharged. 29% of AI-adopting insurers report using no explainability method at all. Sector-wide the figure is worse.

One inconsistency worth knowing before you cite it. The main report's narrative text states that 21% of institutions use no explainability method. Its own Figure 15, on the same page, shows 34%. The two do not reconcile. If you quote a sector-wide figure, quote the chart and say so — or use the insurance-specific 29%, which is internally consistent.

06 — Ownership

And no one owns it

Asked who is accountable for AI decisions, insurers scattered. The Board leads at 25% — meaning three-quarters of insurers locate accountability somewhere other than the Board, and no single role holds even a third of the sector.

07 — What it adds up to

Three things follow

The duty exists and the instrument does not. POPIA s71 and the FAIS automated-advice requirements already oblige an institution to reconstruct and defend a specific automated decision. Fewer than one in five insurers has an auditing framework; nearly one in three cannot explain a model's output at all. The obligation is not waiting on new regulation to arrive.

Validation is not evidence. Banking's profile is the clearest statement of the problem: 91% validate their models, 27% audit them. Validation asks whether a model is fit for purpose in general. Evidence asks what a named system did, in one case, on one date, and whether that account can be trusted by someone who was not there. The survey shows the sector has built the first and not the second.

Insurance is the more credible near-term case than lending. Not because lending's numbers look better — they look worse — but because eight respondents cannot carry an argument. Insurance gives you 59 institutions, a statutory hook in underwriting and claims, and a 56-point gap between what they say and what they have.

Limits of this data
  • Lending is N=8. One institution moves any lending figure by 12.5 points. Directional at best.
  • Self-reported. The report itself flags interpretation bias, optimism bias and incomplete disclosure, particularly on risk management practices.
  • Voluntary, and briefly open. October to December 2024 only — the report notes this may have excluded institutions needing internal approval to respond.
  • Now dated. Fieldwork is roughly two years old; adoption figures in particular will have moved.
  • Fintechs were folded into payments for reporting, which obscures fintech-specific patterns.
  • Percentages are reported to the whole number and several sets do not sum cleanly; treat them as read off charts, not as precise.

What to watch next. The report closes by announcing that the FSCA and PA “will develop a discussion paper that will build on the findings of this joint FSCA–PA AI research report and will engage stakeholders on key regulatory and supervisory questions.” As of August 2026 it has not appeared. That paper is where the sector's supervisory expectations on evidence and explainability will first be put out for comment — the moment before a decision is made, and the point of maximum influence.
